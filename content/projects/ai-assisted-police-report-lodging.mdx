## Overview

SPF's existing online report lodging system required citizens to fill in a standardised form regardless of the type of incident they were reporting. While the system captured basic information, it provided no guidance on what level of detail was needed, resulting in reports that were frequently incomplete or ambiguous. Investigation officers routinely had to follow up with complainants to clarify missing information, reducing their capacity for higher-priority work.

I was brought in to design R-COP (Report Lodging Co-Pilot): an AI-assisted chatbot that guides citizens through the report lodging process conversationally, using a curated question set adapted from existing SPF investigation workflows. R-COP surfaces case-type-specific follow-up questions based on what the citizen has described, prompting for the details officers need for their case investigation, and reducing back and forth between officers and citizens.

## My Role

I was the sole UX designer in a cross-functional team with a product manager, scrum master, and six engineers (including DevOps and AI engineers). I led the end-to-end design process from heuristic analysis through to usability-tested prototypes, and worked directly with SPF stakeholders on design decisions.

## Research & Discovery

### Understanding the existing kiosk report lodging process

Before designing the AI chatbot, I conducted a heuristic analysis of the existing report lodging experience currently deployed at Self-Help Kiosks (SHKs) across Singapore's Electronic Police Centres.

The existing system already used a conversational metaphor: a 3D animated avatar named "Officer Clif" guided users through the process, asking questions in natural language. The flow covered six sections, supported Singpass authentication with MyInfo auto-population, and allowed users to save a draft and return within 7 days.

Walking through the full flow surfaced a few main pain points:

<StatementHeading
  label="Pain point"
  number={1}
  title="Inaccessible colour contrasts and text sizes"
/>

The current UI frequently had inaccessible colour pairing for main CTA buttons, and text sizes that were too small. These were especially critical fixes, considering that the police lodging kiosk would be serving the general public, which may include users with visual impairments.

<StatementHeading
  label="Pain point"
  number={2}
  title="The chatbot was a form in disguise"
/>

The current system was not able to adapt to different case types being lodged. A standard set of questions was posed regardless of the user's initial prompt. This contributed to the operational problem of police officers having to reach out to the complainant to gather more information after the report was lodged.

<StatementHeading
  label="Pain point"
  number={3}
  title="Editing any fields became a difficult process"
/>

After complainants reached the preview stage before submitting their final report, if they wished to edit any of the information they entered, they were redirected to a chat log history, where they had to navigate to the exact chat bubble to be able to make edits. This requires a lot of recall on the user's part.

### Current kiosk user journey

Mapping the end-to-end flow revealed a six-stage journey from kiosk landing to submission: landing and avatar introduction, identity verification (Singpass QR, NRIC, or passport), personal details (MyInfo auto-population), incident details across three sections (what, when, where, who), supporting information (witnesses, CCTV, items), and review and submit.

<Screenshot
  src="/projects/ai-assisted-police-report-lodging/current-kiosk-journey.png"

The critical gap was concentrated in Sections 4 and 5: despite the structured flow, the free-text "What happened?" field consistently produced reports that were either too vague or missing case-type-specific details that investigation officers (IOs) needed to assess and process the case.

## Design

With the research findings in mind, I made a few key design decisions:

<StatementHeading
  label="Design decision"
  number={1}
  title="Including a progress indicator"
/>

I proposed a step-progress sidebar as a core navigation element, giving citizens visibility into how far along they were in the process. This was met with resistance from the engineering team, who raised a concern that exposing the question structure could allow malicious actors to predict and game the AI's logic. I worked with the team to find an acceptable level of breakdown. This preserved user orientation without compromising the system's security.

<StatementHeading
  label="Design decision"
  number={3}
  title="Choosing the right interaction for each question type"
/>

Some questions required open-ended responses (such as incident descriptions), while some required yes/no responses (such as the presence of CCTV cameras). I designed the input components to translate the conversation flow into an interaction system that complainants could interact with easily. Instead of the current system which displays two primary buttons, I used inputs that were more intuitive to understand.

<Screenshot
  src="/projects/ai-assisted-police-report-lodging/input-types.png"
  alt="Input type components mapped to different question types in the conversation flow"
  caption={null}
/>

Checkboxes were used for acknowledgements and questions allowing multiple selections. Radio buttons were used for mutually exclusive options. Free-text input supported open-ended incident descriptions, dropdowns were used for selecting a single option from a list, and structured form fields captured precise data like dates, times, and item values.

## Usability Testing

To validate the redesigned experience, I conducted usability testing with public sector professionals representative of members of the public.

Testing was conducted across two rounds (7 and 9 October 2025) with 9 participants from a cross-section of Singapore public sector professionals with varying levels of digital literacy. Participants were walked through multiple report lodging scenarios (physical assault, scam, theft) to test different chatbot conversation flows.

<Screenshot
  src="/projects/ai-assisted-police-report-lodging/usability-testing.png"
  alt="Bar chart of usability testing scores for 13 report lodging tasks, all rated between 5.0 and 5.7 out of 6, with an overall satisfaction of 5.3"
  caption={null}
/>

During usability testing, participants completed the report lodging flow in approximately 4 minutes on average across the two rounds. For context, the existing system was estimated to take approximately 15 minutes for a comparable submission. The difference in completion time reflects the core design intent: replacing a six-section form with a guided conversational flow that structures the process for the user.

The sessions also uncovered several usability issues and opportunities for refinement, including areas where navigation, terminology, and question sequencing could be further improved. Based on these findings, I iterated on the design before implementation to better align the experience with users' expectations.

## Outcome

R-COP was launched on 9 kiosks across 7 divisional HQ Electronic Police Centres in Oct 2025, with a roadmap to expand to 50% of all kiosks in Singapore by April 2026 and full nationwide rollout by end of 2026. Following deployment, users consistently rated the experience above 4/5 for overall satisfaction, based on feedback collected at the end of each report lodged.

Post-launch, the team's focus shifted to improving the AI model's case classification accuracy. This helps sharpen how well an incident description maps to the right question set, since accurate classification of case types ensures the right question sets are asked. That work was ongoing when I left the project.

## Reflections

<StatementHeading
  label="Reflections"
  number={1}
  title="Designing for uncertainty instead of certainty"
/>

Working on R-COP was my first experience designing for an output I couldn't
fully predict or control. Unlike traditional forms where users populate
predefined fields, the incident report was generated by an LLM. The same
conversation could produce slightly different summaries, and there was always a
possibility that the model might misstate or omit details.

This changed how I approached the design. Rather than treating AI errors as edge cases, I had to assume uncertainty was an inherent part of the experience and make review and editing a core part of the workflow. It also introduced new design questions that don't exist in traditional software, such as how users should verify AI-generated content, when confidence or uncertainty should be surfaced, and how to help users quickly spot potential hallucinations. It reinforced that designing AI products isn't just about the generation itself—it's about helping users build enough trust to confidently use and validate the output.
