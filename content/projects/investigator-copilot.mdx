## Overview

Investigator Copilot is an AI-powered platform built for the Singapore Police Force. I led the redesign of its Speech-to-Text component after inheriting an MVP from an external vendor, transforming it into a workflow that better supports live investigations through clearer interactions, real-time note-taking, and AI-generated summaries.

## Problem

### Manual transcription delays investigations

Interview recordings are typically sent to third-party vendors or specialist officers for manual transcription. Officers often wait 3–10 days before receiving a transcript, delaying internal reviews, time-sensitive consultations, and downstream investigative work.

### Assistant officers are cognitively overloaded during interviews

Assistant officers carry out multiple tasks during an interview — monitoring recording equipment, taking interview notes, and supporting the lead investigator — increasing cognitive load and dividing attention during a critical stage of the investigation. During post-test interviews, 2 of 6 participants identified the need to capture observations during interviews.

### Vendor MVP had usability issues

During usability testing, 2 of 6 users had difficulty marking transcript segments, prompting the need for this interaction to be redesigned.

### Usability testing with MVP

I conducted usability testing with investigation officers using the existing MVP to understand how the interface performed during realistic interview scenarios.

<StatementHeading
  label="Research insight"
  number={1}
  title="Officers couldn't clearly distinguish transcript states"
/>

Users clicked twice because they couldn't tell whether a transcript had already been marked. The hover state would still be applied if the user's cursor was still hovering over the transcript row, causing the transcript row to be unmarked instead if the user clicked again.

<Screenshot
  src="/projects/investigator-copilot/mvp-before-mark.png"
  alt="The MVP's hover and marked states were visually identical"
/>

<StatementHeading
  label="Research insight"
  number={2}
  title="Moving transcription content made interaction difficult"
/>

The transcript continuously shifted during interviews, forcing users to chase moving content whenever they wanted to bookmark an important statement.

<Screenshot
  src="/projects/investigator-copilot/mvp-before-moving-transcript.png"
  alt="The MVP's live transcription screen, labelled Before, with an arrow indicating the transcript rows shifting upward as new content streamed in"
/>

<StatementHeading
  label="Research insight"
  number={3}
  title="Officers need to make notes during interviews"
/>

Officers needed to capture important observations during interviews, making notes throughout the course of the interview, which the current workspace could not allow for.

### Key design decisions

<StatementHeading
  label="Design decision"
  number={1}
  title="Clear transcript states"
/>

To make the hover and marked states more visually distinct, I explored a few options, including changing the colours used to indicate hover/marked states, and changing the interaction of how a transcript can get marked. One other consideration was also to make the clickable area of marking a transcript larger, as the current area was determined by the length of the transcript text.

<Screenshot
  src="/projects/investigator-copilot/hover-marked-exploration-before.png"
  alt="Default, hover, and marked states before the redesign — hovering highlights only the transcript text with a mark tooltip, and marked rows use a yellow text highlight"
  caption=""
/>

<Screenshot
  src="/projects/investigator-copilot/hover-marked-exploration-1.png"
  alt="Iteration 1 of the default, hover, and marked states — a red speaker avatar is added and the text highlight treatments are kept from the original design"
  caption="Users gave feedback about wanting more visual differentiation between different speakers, hence I added an avatar component for each speaker, and defined the rules for how they would appear with the developers. Given that there are only a maximum of 5 speakers, we hardcoded each speaker to a certain colour so that they would all have sufficient colour contrast."
/>

<Screenshot
  src="/projects/investigator-copilot/hover-marked-exploration-2.png"
  alt="Iteration 2 of the default, hover, and marked states — a floating mark button appears beside the row on hover, and a persistent bookmark icon shows on marked rows"
  caption="Usability testing revealed that users couldn't confidently distinguish transcript states. Hence, I made the visual differentiation between hover and marked states more clear, by placing the background fill for hover states over the entire transcript row. I also opted for a persistent bookmark icon once a row was marked, as it was more clearly identifiable. A clickable button element to mark the transcript row instead of text (which varies in length) also enlarges the clickable area, and makes the marking interaction more consistent, improving usability."
/>

<Screenshot
  src="/projects/investigator-copilot/hover-marked-exploration-3.png"
  alt="Iteration 3 of the default, hover, and marked states — the bookmark button sits within the highlighted row on hover, and marked rows keep the highlight with a filled bookmark icon"
  caption="The bookmark button was shifted to be inline with the transcription row, to reduce the vertical padding required, considering the small screen sizes some officers may be working with."
/>

<StatementHeading
  label="Design decision"
  number={2}
  title="Side panel instead of modal"
/>

Investigators need to reference transcripts through the course of the interview, and a modal would block investigators from referring to the current transcript if they wanted to view any of the segments they bookmarked during the interview.

<Screenshot
  src="/projects/investigator-copilot/marked-segments-modal-before.png"
  alt="Viewing marked segments required opening a modal on the original design"
  caption="Clicking on a button opens a modal to viewing marked segments, blocking the view of the current transcript."
/>

<Screenshot
  src="/projects/investigator-copilot/marked-segments-modal-exploration-1.png"
  alt="Iteration 1 of the marked segments side panel — clicking on transcript text bookmarks it, and it appears in a persistent Marked Segments panel as a table row beside the transcript"
  caption="I replaced the modal with a persistent side panel, so officers could view marked segments alongside the live transcript. Clicking on a transcript row bookmarks it, and it appears in the panel as a table row mirroring the transcript layout. This layout also shortens the length of transcription texts, which enhances its readability by reducing the distance traveled by a user's eye before tracking to the next line."
/>

<Screenshot
  src="/projects/investigator-copilot/marked-segments-modal-exploration-2.png"
  alt="Iteration 2 of the marked segments side panel — marked segments appear under a Marked tab as cards with an inline comment field beside the live transcript"
  caption="Through further discussion with users, our team discovered that notes would be a helpful feature for officers who need to work on a case with other officers. The marked panel was refined to share the side panel with the AI-generated summary, accessible by switching between tabs."
/>

<StatementHeading
  label="Design decision"
  number={3}
  title="Supporting live note-taking"
/>

To allow officers to record key observations noted during interviews, a note-taking feature was implemented alongside marked transcript text. This allowed officers to remain on the platform throughout the course of the interview without having to switch between manual note-taking and transcription review. I designed a note-taking feature for each marked segment, so that officers can add, edit, and delete any notes they had through the course of the interview.

<Screenshot
  src="/projects/investigator-copilot/marked-segments-card-states.png"
  alt="Marked segment cards in the new side panel, shown alongside live note-taking"
  caption="Marked segment cards in the new side panel allow users to create, edit and delete notes, to note down observations they have during interviews conducted."
/>

<StatementHeading
  label="Design decision"
  number={4}
  title="Define behaviour for autoscroll"
/>

Usability testing revealed that the moving transcripts made it hard for users to interact with a transcription row. Hence, I added a behaviour for autoscrolling transcripts to be turned off if the user scrolls. A button to allow the user to jump back to the current transcription timing will appear, which will re-enable autoscroll behaviour during recording and playback.

<Screenshot
  src="/projects/investigator-copilot/autoscroll-logic.png"
  alt="Flow diagram of the autoscroll behaviour — autoscroll is enabled by default, becomes disabled when the user manually scrolls, and is re-enabled when the user clicks Jump to Playback"
  caption=" "
/>

<Screenshot

  alt="Live transcription screen with a marked transcript row highlighted and a Jump to Latest button overlaying the transcript, beside a Marked panel listing bookmarked segments with comment fields"
  caption="Example of a button which appears if autoscroll is disabled. Clicking it returns the user to the current transcription timing and re-enables autoscroll."

<StatementHeading
  label="Design decision"
  number={5}
  title="Turning AI output into meaningful participant roles"
/>

The AI could only identify unique voices as Speaker 1, Speaker 2, and Speaker
3 etc. It couldn't determine whether a speaker was the interviewer, witness, or
suspect. To bridge this gap, I designed a post-transcription workflow that
allowed investigators to assign meaningful roles to each speaker. Since speaker
diarisation wasn't always accurate, I also made it easy to reassign speakers
through a dropdown during editing, allowing users to correct AI mistakes before
using the transcript downstream.

<Screenshot
  src="/projects/investigator-copilot/speaker-assigning-flow.png"
  alt="Speaker assigning flow — the edit transcript screen with a Speaker dropdown listing Speaker 1, 2 and 3 as unassigned, leading to an Assign Speaker modal where the user selects a role and enters a name for the speaker"
  caption="Officers can assign a role and name to each detected speaker directly from the transcript"

## Usability Testing

During a usability testing session conducted with the new designs with 11 participants, it achieved a SUS score of 75. Users did not report further issues with bookmarking transcripts, and gave feedback that the improved interface was easier to use.

## Outcome

The product was successfully launched in April 2026, with officers adopting it for their workflows.

The redesigned transcript review workflow supports investigators by:

- providing near-immediate access to interview transcripts instead of waiting for manual transcription
- enabling faster internal case reviews and urgent consultations with stakeholders
- providing a platform for assistant interviewers to record interview notes alongside marked transcription segments
- retaining officers' control over AI-generated transcripts through allowing for reviewing and editing

The team is still gathering feedback as the product goes live, and working on further enhancements.

## Reflections

<StatementHeading
  label="Reflection"
  number={1}
  title="Designing begins at the component level"
/>

One thing I underestimated was how many interaction states a transcript could have simultaneously. What initially appeared to be a list became a system where playback, editing, marking, and scrolling all influenced one another. Designing at the component level before designing screens ultimately reduced complexity and made the interface more manageable.

<Screenshot
  src="/projects/investigator-copilot/transcript-row-component.png"
  alt="The transcript row component, broken down into its interaction states"
/>

<StatementHeading
  label="Reflection"
  number={2}
  title="Designing beyond the happy path"
/>

One of my biggest takeaways from this project was that a polished happy path isn't enough for a successful implementation. As engineers began building the product, many discussions centred on scenarios that hadn't been explicitly designed—empty states, unexpected data lengths, disabled actions, and other edge cases that inevitably occur in production.

That experience changed the way I approach design handoffs. I now work through a structured checklist before every handoff, reviewing not only the primary workflow but also loading, empty, error, and disabled states, atypical data, and interaction edge cases. While not every scenario is implemented immediately, considering them early helps uncover product decisions sooner, reduces ambiguity for engineers, and results in more robust designs.
