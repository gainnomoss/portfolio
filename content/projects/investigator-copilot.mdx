## Overview

Investigator Copilot is an AI-powered platform built for the Singapore Police Force. I led the redesign of its Speech-to-Text component after inheriting an MVP from an external vendor, transforming it into a workflow that better supports live investigations through clearer interactions, real-time note-taking, and AI-assisted review.

## The Problem

Every investigative interview generates hours of audio that must be reviewed and documented accurately. Traditionally, officers either waited several days for outsourced transcription or attempted to take notes manually during interviews, often dividing their attention between listening and documenting.

An MVP transcription tool had already been developed by an external vendor, but usability testing revealed that it introduced new friction rather than reducing it. Officers struggled to keep up with live transcripts, important interactions were difficult to discover, and the interface lacked support for the way investigations are actually conducted.

## My Role

I was the sole product designer responsible for the Speech-to-Text experience after taking over an MVP delivered by an external vendor. I conducted usability testing, redesigned key workflows, created reusable UI components, and partnered closely with one product manager and five engineers through implementation.

## Research & Discovery

I conducted moderated usability testing with investigation officers using the existing MVP to understand how the interface performed during realistic interview scenarios.

<StatementHeading
  label="Research insight"
  number={1}
  title="Officers couldn't confidently distinguish transcript states"
/>

Hover and selection shared similar visual treatments. Users frequently clicked twice because they couldn't tell whether a transcript had already been marked.

<Screenshot
  src="/projects/investigator-copilot/mvp-before-mark.png"
  alt="The MVP's hover and marked states were visually identical"
/>

<StatementHeading
  label="Research insight"
  number={2}
  title="Live transcription made interaction difficult"
/>

The transcript continuously shifted during interviews, forcing users to chase moving content whenever they wanted to bookmark an important statement.

<StatementHeading
  label="Research insight"
  number={3}
  title="Investigation isn't just transcription"
/>

Officers needed to capture observations, identify admissions, rename speakers, and summarize interviews. The transcript alone wasn't enough.

## Design Process

I then studied transcription apps, such as Otter, Dovetail, and Transcribe, to identify common design patterns and layouts to learn from what was already out there.

### Key design decisions

<StatementHeading
  label="Design decision"
  number={1}
  title="Clear transcript states"
/>

To make the hover and marked states more visually distinct, I explored a few options, including changing the colours used to indicate hover/marked states, and changing the interaction of how a transcript can get marked. One consideration was to make the clickable area of marking a transcript larger, as the current area was determined by the length of the transcript text.

<StatementHeading
  label="Design decision"
  number={2}
  title="Side panel instead of modal"
/>

Investigators constantly reference transcripts through the course of the interview, and a modal would block investigators from referring to the current transcript if they wanted to view any of the segments they bookmarked during the interview.

<Screenshot

  caption="I replaced the modal with a persistent side panel, so officers could view marked segments alongside the live transcript. Clicking on a transcript row bookmarks it, and it appears in the panel as a table row mirroring the transcript layout."
/>

<Screenshot
  src="/projects/investigator-copilot/marked-segments-modal-exploration-2.png"
  alt="Iteration 2 of the marked segments side panel — marked segments appear under a Marked tab as cards with an inline comment field beside the live transcript"

I considered using an overlay modal because it required minimal layout changes. However, officers frequently referenced transcripts while reviewing marked evidence, forcing them to repeatedly open and close the modal. I instead explored a persistent side panel, by reducing the length of the transcription text containers, which also improves readability through make the length of texts shorter.

<StatementHeading
  label="Design decision"
  number={3}
  title="Supporting live note-taking"
/>

To allow officers to record key observations noted during interviews, a note-taking feature was implemented alongside marked transcript text. This allowed officers to remain on the platform throughout the course of the interview without having to switch between manual note-taking and transcription review. I designed a note-taking features for each marked segment, so that officers can add, edit, and delete any notes they had through the course of the interivew.

## Usability Testing

During a usability testing session conducted with the new designs with 11 participants, it achieved a SUS score of 75. Users did not report further issues with bookmarking transcripts, and gave feedback that the improved interface was easier to use.

## Outcome

The product was launched in April 2026, with officers adopting it for their workflows.

## Reflections

<StatementHeading
  label="Reflection"
  number={1}
  title="Designing begins at the component level"
/>

One thing I underestimated was how many interaction states a transcript could have simultaneously. What initially appeared to be a list became a system where playback, editing, marking, and scrolling all influenced one another. Designing at the component level before designing screens ultimately reduced complexity and made the interface more scalable.

<Screenshot
  src="/projects/investigator-copilot/transcript-row-component.png"
  alt="The transcript row component, broken down into its interaction states"
/>

<StatementHeading
  label="Reflection"
  number={2}
  title="Balancing user needs, technical limitations, and stakeholder priorities"
/>

Designing for investigators reinforced that productivity tools are rarely about adding more features — they're about reducing cognitive effort during high-pressure work. Working within government constraints also taught me the importance of balancing user needs, technical limitations, and stakeholder priorities while still advocating for better user experiences.
