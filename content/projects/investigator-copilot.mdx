## Overview

Investigator Copilot is an AI-powered platform built for the Singapore Police Force. I led the redesign of its Speech-to-Text component after inheriting an MVP from an external vendor, transforming it into a workflow that better supports live investigations through clearer interactions, real-time note-taking, and AI-generated summaries.

## Background

I inherited a working MVP from an external vendor when I joined this project. It gave officers a way to record and review interview transcripts, but the interactions hadn't been refined for how officers actually worked day-to-day.

<Screenshot
  src="/projects/investigator-copilot/background-vendor-mvp.png"
  alt="The vendor's original Complete screen: a static transcript table with speaker labels S1, S2 and S3, and no way to mark, annotate or search through the transcript"
  caption="The MVP I inherited from the vendor, before the redesign"
/>

## Problem

### Manual transcription delays investigations

Interview recordings are typically sent to third-party vendors or specialist officers for manual transcription. Officers often wait 3–10 days before receiving a transcript, delaying internal reviews, time-sensitive consultations, and downstream investigative work.

### Assistant officers are cognitively overloaded during interviews

Assistant officers carry out multiple tasks during an interview — monitoring recording equipment, taking interview notes, and supporting the lead investigator — increasing cognitive load and dividing attention during a critical stage of the investigation.

### The vendor MVP hadn't been validated with officers

The MVP I inherited had not been tested with the officers who would actually use it, creating usability risks ahead of a wider rollout.

### Usability testing with the inherited MVP

I conducted usability testing with 6 officers across 2 sessions, followed by post-test interviews to better understand their workflows and pain points.

<StatementHeading
  label="Research insight"
  number={1}
  title="Officers had difficulty marking transcript segments"
/>

During usability testing, 2 of 6 users had difficulty marking transcript segments — for two compounding reasons. The hover and marked states were visually near-identical, so users often clicked twice, unintentionally unmarking a segment they meant to keep. On top of that, the transcript kept shifting as new content streamed in, forcing users to chase moving content whenever they tried to bookmark a statement.

/>

<StatementHeading
  label="Research insight"
  number={2}
  title="Officers need to make notes during interviews"
/>

Officers needed to capture important observations throughout an interview, which the current workspace didn't support. 2 of 6 users specifically mentioned note-taking as part of their existing workflow.

<Screenshot

/>

<StatementHeading
  label="Research insight"
  number={3}
  title="Speaker diarisation was not always accurate"
/>

Despite engineering efforts to improve diarisation, the AI didn't always tell speakers apart correctly. In one interview, it correctly labelled an officer's opening question, but then attributed a later question from the same officer to the interviewee, collapsing two people into a single "Speaker 2" thread. This eroded officers' trust in the transcript, and became a key input for how I approached AI-generated content downstream: never treat it as ground truth.

### Key design decisions

<StatementHeading
  label="Design decision"
  number={1}
  title="Clear transcript states"
/>

To make the hover and marked states more visually distinct, I explored a few options, including changing the colours used to indicate hover/marked states, and changing the interaction of how a transcript can get marked. One other consideration was also to make the clickable area of marking a transcript larger, as the current area was determined by the length of the transcript text.

<StatementHeading
  label="Design decision"
  number={3}
  title="Supporting live note-taking"
/>

To allow officers to record key observations noted during interviews, a note-taking feature was implemented alongside marked transcript text. This allowed officers to remain on the platform throughout the course of the interview without having to switch between manual note-taking and transcription review. I designed a note-taking feature for each marked segment, so that officers can add, edit, and delete any notes they had through the course of the interview.

<StatementHeading
  label="Design decision"
  number={4}
  title="Define behaviour for autoscroll"
/>

Usability testing revealed that the moving transcripts made it hard for users to interact with a transcription row. Hence, I added a behaviour for autoscrolling transcripts to be turned off if the user scrolls. A button to allow the user to jump back to the current transcription timing will appear, which will re-enable autoscroll behaviour during recording and playback.

<StatementHeading
  label="Design decision"
  number={5}
  title="Turning AI output into meaningful participant roles"
/>

The AI could only identify unique voices as Speaker 1, Speaker 2, and Speaker
3 etc. It couldn't determine whether a speaker was the interviewer, witness, or
suspect. To bridge this gap, I designed a post-transcription workflow that
allowed investigators to assign meaningful roles to each speaker. Since speaker
diarisation wasn't always accurate, I also made it easy to reassign speakers
through a dropdown during editing, allowing users to correct AI mistakes before
using the transcript downstream.

<Screenshot
  src="/projects/investigator-copilot/speaker-edit-flows.png"
  alt="Three edit flows branching from the Complete screen: editing a speaker via an Assign Speaker modal, editing a timestamp inline, and editing transcript text inline"
  caption="Officers can correct the assigned speaker, timestamp, or transcript text directly, fixing AI mistakes before using the transcript downstream"
/>

<StatementHeading
  label="Design decision"
  number={6}
  title="Designing for failure states"
/>

Live transcription depends on a stable mic and connection throughout an interview — something officers can't always guarantee inside an interview room. I designed dedicated error states for the two most disruptive failures: a disconnected microphone, and a lost connection mid-session, each with a clear recovery path back into the transcription flow.

<Screenshot

## Usability Testing

During a usability testing session conducted with the new designs with 11 participants, it achieved a SUS score of 75, rated "Good" on the SUS scale. Users did not report further issues with bookmarking transcripts, and gave feedback that the improved interface was easier to use.

## Outcome

The product was successfully launched in April 2026, with officers adopting it for their workflows.

The redesigned transcript review workflow supports investigators by:

- providing near-immediate access to interview transcripts instead of waiting for manual transcription
- enabling faster internal case reviews and urgent consultations with stakeholders
- providing a platform for assistant interviewers to record interview notes alongside marked transcription segments
- retaining officers' control over AI-generated transcripts through allowing for reviewing and editing

The team is still gathering feedback as the product goes live, and working on further enhancements.

## Reflections

<StatementHeading
  label="Reflection"
  number={1}
  title="Designing begins at the component level"
/>

One thing I underestimated was how many interaction states a transcript could have simultaneously. What initially appeared to be a list became a system where playback, editing, marking, and scrolling all influenced one another. Designing at the component level before designing screens ultimately reduced complexity and made the interface more manageable.

<Screenshot
  src="/projects/investigator-copilot/transcript-row-component.png"
  alt="The transcript row component, broken down into its interaction states"
/>

<StatementHeading
  label="Reflection"
  number={2}
  title="Designing beyond the happy path"
/>

One of my biggest takeaways from this project was that a polished happy path isn't enough for a successful implementation. As engineers began building the product, many discussions centred on scenarios that hadn't been explicitly designed—empty states, unexpected data lengths, disabled actions, and other edge cases that inevitably occur in production.

That experience changed the way I approach design handoffs. I now work through a structured checklist before every handoff, reviewing not only the primary workflow but also loading, empty, error, and disabled states, atypical data, and interaction edge cases. The failure states I designed for microphone and connection loss above are one direct result of that shift. While not every scenario is implemented immediately, considering them early helps uncover product decisions sooner, reduces ambiguity for engineers, and results in more robust designs.
